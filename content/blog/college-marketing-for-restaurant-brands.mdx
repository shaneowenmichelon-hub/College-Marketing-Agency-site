## What does a campus dining hall change?

Campus dining is an operator's building, not a public sidewalk. Ask that campus, in writing, who runs the halls and whether an outside restaurant may sample, sell, or set up a station inside them. Do not assume a national dining contract covers the school you want.

Keep four answers with the brief: who signs for an outside food brand, whether a sample may be handed out or only displayed, which tender the register accepts, and whether that yes names one hall or every food door. A yes that does not name the door is not a yes.

Meal plans and dining dollars are that operator's tender. A coupon your app already reads is not a swipe on their register. A yes for one hall is not a yes for the student union or a second campus. If the answer is no, move the sample to a permitted quad, a student-organization event with its own written yes, or an off-campus store a student can reach.

## How should a coupon, a delivery zone, or a grand opening be counted?

Put one code on the offer, and put the campus on that code, when you need to tell schools apart. Collegiate Agency will place the code you send. It will not invent a code, and it will not treat a shared national code as a campus result.

Name the off-campus boundary your delivery app already uses. A flyer handed out on the quad does not prove the order came from that school. Do not ask the crew to guess a radius. If the zone does not reach the dorms, say so in the brief and do not promise that it does.

A grand opening near campus is still the city's event. If the line, the tent, or the sampling team stands on school property, add that school's vendor or tabling permit. Those rules differ by school. A store off campus does not borrow the dining hall's permission, and a dining-hall yes does not cover the sidewalk in front of the new store.

## What food permit should the brief name?

The U.S. Food and Drug Administration posted [Food Code 2022](https://www.fda.gov/food/fda-food-code/food-code-2022) on December 28, 2022. That page says the most recent version is the January 18, 2023 version, and that the Food Code is a model offered for adoption by local, state, and federal jurisdictions. It is not the permit for your campus, and it does not say every campus has adopted that text.

In that January 18, 2023 file, the examples include a restaurant and giving food to a consumer directly or through a delivery service such as restaurant takeout. A temporary food establishment operates for no more than 14 consecutive days with a single event or celebration. Read those definitions in the [Food Code 2022 full document](https://www.fda.gov/files/food/published/FDA-FoodCode2022-FullDocument-01182023_0.pdf). Ask before you treat the model as the local code.

King County, Washington, published [Temporary Food Establishment Guidelines and Fee Schedules](https://cdn.kingcounty.gov/-/media/king-county/depts/dph/documents/certificates-permits-licenses/food-worker-business-permits/temporary-food-establishment-guidelines.pdf) dated January 2026. Packaged foods that need time and temperature control, with no sampling, sit in a different permit category from sampling those foods. The sheet bars home storage and home preparation. Its examples of an approved commissary are a restaurant, a church, a school, or a community center. It bars bare-hand contact with ready-to-eat food and calls for tongs, papers, or gloves. Hot holding is 135°F or above. Cold holding is 41°F or below. The application is due at least 14 days before the event. That lead time is the county's application clock, not the Food Code's limit on how long a temporary establishment may operate. Fees on the sheet are King County's, not a Collegiate Agency price. Put the sheet for the city you are actually in into the brief.

Do not portion food in a dorm or an apartment. If the stop only hands out a manufacturer's sealed package, say so, because some health departments treat that differently from food portioned on site. Collegiate Agency will not open a table until the permit name is on the sheet.

## How should a paid student food post disclose the relationship?

The Federal Trade Commission adopted revised [Guides Concerning the Use of Endorsements and Testimonials in Advertising](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2023-07-26/html/2023-14795.htm) on July 26, 2023. The notice says the guides were effective that day. A payment, a free or discounted product, or the chance of a prize has to be disclosed when a significant minority of viewers are likely unaware of it. Clear and conspicuous means difficult to miss and easily understood. A disclosure that sits only on a profile page is easy to miss. If the post is visible without a "more" tap and the disclosure is not, the guides say that disclosure is not unavoidable. The guides also describe a college student who earns points for posts and can exchange those points for prizes such as concert tickets or electronics. Those incentives have to be disclosed, and the advertiser should check that the disclosure is on the post.

A free meal for a post is a free product. Put one sentence in the brief, in the language of the post, and say where it sits. Collegiate Agency checks the live post against that sentence. Your counsel owns the wording, including any price or allergen line.

## What should a restaurant brand refuse in a campus proposal?

Refuse one national price. Refuse a reach number with no campus on it. Refuse a redemption forecast copied from another company's campus work. Refuse a dining-hall sample, a branded station, or a meal-plan tender without the dining operator's written yes. Refuse food portioned in a dorm, an apartment, or any kitchen the health department has not approved. Refuse a code your own system cannot read. Refuse a student club billed at $1,500 per sorority (chapter) when it is not a sorority (chapter).
