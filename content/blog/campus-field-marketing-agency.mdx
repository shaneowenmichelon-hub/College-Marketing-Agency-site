A campus field marketing agency should give you control over what happens between shipping product and receiving the recap. If you manage a brand's field budget, you need more than a campus list and a promise of student enthusiasm. You need a named operator, an approved location, a credible inventory trail, and evidence that helps you decide whether to expand.

That distinction matters when you are planning a spring semester test or evaluating a new partner before a larger rollout. A small pilot can expose weak handoffs without committing your entire program. It can also show which local differences deserve accommodation and which execution standards must remain consistent. Use the buying framework below to compare proposals on the work you can inspect, not on the confidence of the pitch.

## Buy a defined field assignment, not a campus footprint

Start your brief with a single assignment. For example, ask for an approved product trial experience in selected campus markets, supported by trained local staff and a reconciled inventory report. This is an illustrative scope, not a promise about what every school permits. Name the product, intended audience, activation setting, calendar constraints, and the action you want a participant to take.

Separate confirmed access from proposed access. A vendor may know students in a market without having permission to set up on university property. Ask which party grants approval, what is included in that permission, and when it must be secured. A nearby venue, student apartment property, and official university event are different placements. Your proposal should not treat them as interchangeable.

If the plan depends on an existing event audience, evaluate the specific [campus event activation options](/services/events) alongside the field staffing scope. Buying a sponsorship does not automatically buy distribution rights, storage, photography access, or permission to collect participant information. Put each dependency in writing before you compare costs. The [campus brand activation agency checklist](/insights/campus-brand-activation-agency) is the companion for format, proof, and staffing questions that sit beside a field pilot.

Give the pilot a boundary your team can enforce. Specify what is excluded, including additional schools, unscheduled shifts, paid media, custom fabrication, or extra product deliveries. An attractive headline fee becomes less useful if every operational requirement appears later as a change order. Compare that fee with the published [ambassador, placement, and event rates](/pricing) before you treat an estimate as the full cost.

## Test local ownership before you approve the roster

Your account contact and your field supervisor do different jobs. Ask who checks the location, receives the shipment, briefs the team, confirms arrival, and resolves a missing shift. You should know which decisions the local supervisor can make and which require your approval. A roster without this operating structure transfers management work back to your brand team.

For repeated peer-to-peer activity, compare [managed campus brand ambassador support](/services/brand-ambassadors) with temporary event staffing. The right choice depends on whether you need a single supervised shift or continuity across the semester. Do not assume a student creator, ambassador, and field lead can cover one another's responsibilities without training and a revised scope.

Request a rehearsal of one ordinary failure. What happens if the shipment arrives after the scheduled shift? A useful answer names the person who checks delivery, the cutoff for rescheduling, the approved alternative, and the notification you receive. "We will figure it out locally" is not an escalation plan.

Check the academic calendar for the actual campuses under consideration. Exam periods, breaks, move-in dates, and organization schedules can differ. Your local team should explain the proposed timing rather than importing a national calendar. For a spring pilot, work backward from permissions and shipping deadlines, not from the date your internal presentation happens to be due.

## Make the product handoff auditable

The simplest operational control is a shared inventory record. It should distinguish units shipped, units received, units approved for distribution, units handed out, damaged units, and leftovers. Those categories answer different questions. A warehouse shipment is not proof of student trial, and a photograph of a full table does not establish how much product moved.

Assign custody at each handoff. Name who signs for cartons, where stock is stored, who has access, and how the team records a transfer between locations. For products with storage, ingredient, age, or handling restrictions, ask your own compliance team to approve the process. Do not assume a campus marketer can substitute for product-specific or local legal advice.

If you are also exploring [campus product placement](/services#product-placement), keep those units separate from staffed sampling inventory. Product delivered to a partner organization or retail location follows a different path from product handed to participants at a supervised activation. Combining the totals makes both channels harder to assess.

You can keep the records lightweight. A dated receiving confirmation, a shift-level distribution sheet, and a closing reconciliation may be enough for a small test. The important requirement is that every number has a definition, an owner, and a supporting record. Resolve unexplained differences before treating a recap as final.

## Put acceptance criteria into the pilot brief

Use the table below as a starting point for procurement conversations. These are recommended controls, not claims about universal campus rules or guaranteed outcomes. Adapt them to your product and setting, then ask each agency to price the same version of the brief.

| Workstream | Evidence to request | Acceptance question | Owner to name |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Location readiness | Written approval and setup instructions | Is the proposed activity actually permitted here? | Agency lead |
| Staffing | Role list, briefing record, shift confirmation | Did the approved roles receive training and attend? | Field supervisor |
| Product custody | Receiving record and closing inventory | Can you reconcile distribution, waste, and leftovers? | Inventory lead |
| Participant action | Defined counts or consented tracking records | Does the evidence measure the action in the brief? | Brand measurement lead |
| Content delivery | Asset list, permissions, approved usage | Can the brand use each delivered asset as agreed? | Content lead |
| Closeout | Issue log, exceptions, final recap | Are unresolved items visible before expansion? | Account lead |

Attach a short decision checklist to the table:

- Confirm which costs are fixed, estimated, or passed through.
- Assign a deadline for approving locations and creative.
- Define the conditions that trigger cancellation or relocation.
- Specify the format and due date of the recap.
- Identify who accepts the work and who can approve added scope.

Do not turn every line into a performance guarantee. An agency can commit to staffing, approved setup, reporting, and agreed delivery standards. Student participation still depends on the setting, offer, product, and other factors. Separate execution obligations from experimental targets so the contract does not reward inflated reporting.

## Measure the pilot without confusing activity and results

Build a short measurement chain: what the team did, what participants did, and what your business can reasonably infer. Staffed hours and distributed units describe execution. Scans, opt-ins, or redemptions may describe a next step, provided the collection method is approved and reliable. None of those numbers automatically proves incremental revenue.

Agree on definitions before launch. Does a "conversation" require a product explanation, or does a quick greeting count? Does a scan represent a unique visitor? Are repeat participants excluded? If campuses use different definitions, a comparison table can look precise while saying very little. Require the agency to flag estimates and missing data rather than filling gaps with assumptions.

When paid or gifted student content supports the field activity, include disclosure review in the brief. The [FTC's guidance on influencer disclosures](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/disclosures-101-social-media-influencers) explains that material brand relationships should be clear and that disclosures belong with the endorsement. It also cautions against claims about products someone has not tried. Use that guidance to inform review; it does not verify any particular campaign's compliance or results.

Finally, write the expansion rule before you see the recap. You might require complete operational records, acceptable execution quality, and enough observed participant response to justify another test. Set your own thresholds based on the economics of your product. If results are mixed, repeat one variable deliberately rather than adding campuses and hoping the uncertainty disappears.

A useful pilot ends with a decision: expand, revise, or stop. Ask the agency to recommend what should change and identify the evidence behind that recommendation.
